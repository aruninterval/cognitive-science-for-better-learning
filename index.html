Why we forget (and why it feels fine)

Memory fades fast without effort to retrieve it. The surprise: the study methods that feel smoothest are often the weakest.

Strategy 1 of 5

Retrieval practice

Pulling information out of memory strengthens it far more than putting it in again.

Try it now: brain dump

Without scrolling up, write 2–3 things you remember from Lesson 1.

Key ideas from Lesson 1Memory fades quickly without review · Re-reading creates false confidence · Effortful recall is a good sign.

You just did retrieval practice. That small effort of remembering is exactly what makes the memory last.

How to use it

  1. Close the materialPut the notes away before you test yourself.
  2. Recall in your own wordsWrite, say or sketch what you remember.

Strategy 2 of 5

Spaced practice

Same total study time, spread out, beats one big cram session for long-term memory.

Study sessionExam / real use

Rule of thumbReview soon after learning, then again after a day, a few days, a week. Gaps get longer as the memory strengthens.
Strategy 3 of 5

Interleaving

Mix related topics or problem types instead of finishing one before starting the next.

Interleaving forces you to choose which method applies, which is the skill you need in real life.

Strategy 4 of 5

Dual coding

Pair words with relevant visuals. Two memory routes beat one.

What counts as a useful visual?
Diagrams, timelines, flows, labelled sketches, charts. They show relationships and structure.
What doesn't?
Decorative stock photos. If the image doesn't explain something, it adds load instead of learning.
A quick way to apply it
After reading, close the page and draw the idea from memory (a mind map, flowchart or sketch), then compare with the source.
Strategy 5 of 5

Manage cognitive load

Working memory holds only a few items at once. Chunk and simplify so effort goes to learning, not decoding.

Try remembering this number

4 9 5 2 8 1 7 3 6 0

Ten separate digits are hard. Three chunks are manageable.
